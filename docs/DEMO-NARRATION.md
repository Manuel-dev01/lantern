# Five-minute product demo narration

This is the tracked narration master for the 05:00 walkthrough. Delivery should
be calm and natural, with room for the on-screen holds. If a synthetic voice is
used, disclose it; do not present it as a human recording.

Most generated memories end as images. Lantern asks a different question: what
if the memory became somewhere you could enter?

This is a real Lantern gift. The recipient arrives at a quiet threshold, sees
their name, and chooses when to step inside. There is no dashboard to understand
and no account to create. The first experience is simply receiving a place that
somebody remembered for you.

Beyond the door is a navigable room. This sewing room is built from a written
memory, reconstructed as a Gaussian-splat environment, and combined with
separate three-dimensional objects. It is not a flat panorama. The viewer can
look around, walk through the space, and inspect the details that make the
memory personal.

Creating one starts on the landing page. Lantern explains the proposition in
one sentence: build someone the place they remember. The room behind that
sentence is an actual world, not an illustrative mock-up. On capable desktop
hardware it moves gently behind the interface; on constrained devices the page
remains complete without it.

The creation flow is deliberately small. First, name the person. Next, describe
the place from memory: the light, the arrangement, the objects, and the feeling
that made it theirs. Finally, add the sender's name. Lantern turns those inputs
into a shareable gift link. This demonstration stops before claiming a new
transaction or generation result; the finished rooms shown here are existing,
verified gifts.

Underneath, two systems do different jobs. World Labs creates the remembered
environment. Tripo creates the individual objects that should feel present
inside it. Lantern assembles both into one browser experience.

That experience opens in stages. A lighter one-hundred-thousand Gaussian preview
appears first, so the recipient has visual context quickly. The interface
explicitly says that the room is still enhancing underneath. When the
full-resolution capture is ready, Lantern swaps it into the same camera position.
The transition improves detail without making the person begin again.

The generated objects remain independent. Here, the sewing machine, fabric,
tomato, and small tools sit inside the captured room. Because they are real
meshes, Lantern can place them, rotate them, and let the viewer approach them.
Placement is checked against the world's collision geometry so an object rests
on a supported surface instead of floating, clipping, or hanging over an edge.

Depth is what makes the composite believable. In the normal view, an object can
disappear behind the table as the camera moves. In this labelled editorial
evidence view, the hidden collider is visible. It is not part of the final
aesthetic; it shows the geometry Lantern uses for walking, seating objects, and
occlusion.

The same collision data also keeps the camera grounded. Spawn points are tested
against the real floor before a gift is published. The viewer should arrive
standing in the room, not outside its captured volume and never falling through
it.

Sharing returns to the emotional idea. A Lantern gift begins with a name and a
door. The sender does not deliver a technical scene or an asset folder. They
send one link, and the recipient decides when to enter.

The constellation collects those gifts without exposing their private memories
as a feed. Each card is a place made for somebody, by somebody. Together they
show that Lantern is not a single generated demo room. It is a format for giving
remembered places.

Everything shown in the browser frame came from the working product or from a
clearly labelled debug view. The large presentation cursor is reconstructed for
readability; it is not offered as evidence of a click. No recipient reaction,
payment, or successful generation has been fabricated.

Lantern combines a simple creation flow, progressive spatial loading, grounded
object placement, and a recipient-first reveal. The result is not just a
generated image. It is a place they can walk through—and a memory someone can
choose to give.
